The Embers of a Dream

Nov 5, 2026
Only recently did I realize that, at last, I am no longer in high school in my dreams. Absurdly, for more than two years before this, I seem never to have thought of myself as a university student while asleep. The things that happened in Shanghai, the people I met, the work I did—once I fell asleep, none of it had ever happened. I was still a high school student in Mudanjiang. There was no university, no afterward, not even a moment’s doubt about who I was. I don’t know whether to call this nostalgia or some kind of mental illness. And of all places, high school. I hated that place, and still do. Schools in the Northeast are hardly hell on earth; at least, compared with those notoriously oppressive provinces, what I went through is scarcely worth mentioning. But what is there to be grateful for? Take the best years of a person’s life, cut them into forty-five-minute pieces, put their freedom, desires, likes, and dislikes under uniform administration—and then, because the guards here happen to be relatively gentle, they are supposed to acknowledge an enormous debt of gratitude. But what came afterward? The timetable no longer decided my life. Everyone was so very free. Free to come and go, free to drift apart, free to maintain necessary or unnecessary connections in countless chat windows. Mature human society has its elaborate etiquette: everyone understands without saying, and everyone must wish everyone else a brilliant future. Of course I like it here. I just sometimes wonder whether the freedom we fought so hard to win ever included the right to be close to other people. Before, we were forced to be together. Now, no one has to be with you. And loneliness, then—is it freedom’s complimentary gift? Erich Fromm wrote a book called Escape from Freedom. I had known that title for a long time, yet never imagined that escaping freedom could be something I did myself. I find it nauseating. The nausea even reaches beyond personal life, into those parts of history people have always been unwilling to understand. Nazi rallies, militarist frenzy, thousands upon thousands of ordinary people surrendering their judgment to grand narratives. How can people be so willing to be enslaved, and then become so practiced at enslaving others? Perhaps I have no right to ask. How easily people fall in love with a life arranged for them, even willingly assigning morality to the arrangement. What everyone endures is no longer called oppression; what everyone does need not be questioned as right or wrong. A life that requires no judgment of one’s own can, in time, even be held up as a virtue. The collective gives people dignity, belonging, a reason not to face themselves alone. All one has to do is follow, and for a while one is spared responsibility for oneself. Of course there are also fear, violence, interests, and countless historical causes that must not be brushed aside. But I think that if our attachment to order were not so deeply rooted, those absurd political formations might not have found it so easy to turn ordinary lives into the material of evil. And I am one of those ordinary people. So the very things I fiercely opposed could still comfort me in my sleep. Why despise the crowd? The people a crowd never lacks are those who believe they do not belong to it. Thinking of this, I begin to understand why humanity always seems to need a temple, a flag, some beliefs that need not be questioned. Who would want to face the world alone at every moment? But now I no longer dream of high school. Something very strange suddenly seems not so strange after all. I am studying artificial intelligence at Shanghai Jiao Tong University now. It sounds promising. A prestigious university, a good major, some future that seems likely to come true: enough to fool a person into believing they are growing upward. When someone asks, I even feel a little secret vanity. That is rather interesting. After all, I am not particularly devoted to worldly achievement. My life is actually very good. A baseball field in the sun, the drums in a live music venue, occasionally going all the way to a distant lake to watch meteors, eating and drinking with friends, working out, traveling—hours that require no reckoning of gains and losses afterward. Work is work; do it, and that is enough. The rest of the time is there to spend as I please. There are so many things a person can do while alive. Why must we always extract some meaning we can account for to the outside world? Besides, the world is so large, and full of useless, lovable things. I have no intention of finding some grand justification for my happiness. Happiness is happiness. It needs no necessary purpose, still less to be converted afterward into some kind of growth. Yet those few worldly labels still give me peace of mind. How very little backbone I have. I do not want to live by their standards, do not want to compromise my likes and dislikes for them. I even look rather skeptically at lives spent endlessly pursuing success and excellence. I do not believe, yet I still partake of its incense offerings. It seems people are not necessarily very loyal to what they profess to believe. This does not make me doubt my life. Those real joys never needed anyone to vouch for them. It is only that things I had long since dismissed in reason have, without my noticing, continued to occupy some part of me. My contempt for them is real, and so is the comfort I take from them. The two even coexisted peacefully, until a dream made them visible. In Homeward Bound, Tao Yuanming writes: “Since I have made my heart serve my body, why be disconsolate and grieve alone?” But when can a person ever know that their heart is not serving something? You can refuse to bow for five pecks of rice, disregard fame and rank, even laugh off other people’s opinions. But what are you to do with the attachments that remain, forced on you by no one and perhaps known to no one? I don’t know. Perhaps nothing needs to be done. People are not clear and immaculate things, after all. Our emotions, our desires, the habits we fail to notice: all of them are ourselves. But I still dislike it—dislike that even while resisting those things, I quietly ask them for comfort. I am no longer a high school student. But I don’t know whether the university student in my dreams now is a free person, or has once again been lulled to sleep by a few beautiful names.

In that essay I had already tried to discuss life’s endpoint, and believed without hesitation that only when a person became truly composed and openhearted could they decide where their life should end.

Confucius said that at seventy he could follow his heart’s desires. Perhaps this means that seventy percent of life’s road has been traveled. Clearly this is a product of utopian thinking. When you really reach an age close to death, every extra day is a gain—who cares about an endpoint?

So, to put it differently, could we replace life with any kind of process?

For example, seventy percent of a day has passed: it is now 16:48. Or you are at the end of the 560th meter of an 800-meter run. Or in week twelve of an eighteen-week semester. So an endpoint and composure can coexist, but only at the endpoint do we know we have both, and acquire the right to choose whether to end.

01Coffee · Almond · Cream

Orgasm

One serving · Rocks glass · Over ice

A sweet, creamy cocktail with several recipes under the same name. This is the equal-parts version with coffee liqueur, amaretto and Irish cream, served over ice for slow sipping.

In the glass

Coffee liqueur, such as Kahlúa
25 ml
Amaretto almond-flavoured liqueur
25 ml
Irish cream liqueur, such as Baileys
25 ml

Equal parts, 1 : 1 : 1. Ice is additional; no extra syrup is needed.

How to make it

  1. Chill a rocks glass and fill it with fresh ice. Measure the three liqueurs into a shaker.
  2. Add plenty of ice and shake for about 10 seconds, until the shaker feels cold and the ingredients are evenly mixed.
  3. Strain over the fresh ice. Serve promptly; a thin layer of foam is normal, and no garnish is necessary.

Flavour & texture

Coffee and roasted aromas meet the almond or marzipan character of amaretto. Irish cream rounds the texture and brings creamy vanilla notes; caramel or cocoa may also come to mind, depending on the brands. Expect sweetness first, with coffee bitterness and gentle alcohol warmth in the finish.

How to taste it

  1. Look for an even pale-coffee colour and a smooth texture. Smell gently near the rim to separate coffee, almond and cream.
  2. Take a small sip and let it coat the mouth briefly. Notice whether sweetness overwhelms the coffee and whether the cream feels silky or heavy.
  3. Return after a little ice has melted. Compare body, sweetness and the length of the coffee finish; water between sips helps reset the palate.

A small adjustment

For a lighter texture, try an extra 10–15 ml of milk on a later attempt and record it as a variation. Avoid adding citrus juice to the cream mixture: it can curdle. Adding vodka produces a different variation, often called a Screaming Orgasm.

02Agave · Lime · Orange

Margarita

One serving · Chilled cocktail glass · Served up

Tequila, orange liqueur and fresh lime make a bright, brisk sour. This is the IBA recipe, shaken and served up; the optional half salt rim lets you taste the drink with and without salt.

In the glass

100% agave tequila; blanco works well
50 ml
Triple sec orange liqueur, such as Cointreau
20 ml
Freshly squeezed lime juice
15 ml

IBA ratio: 50 : 20 : 15. Ice for shaking; a half salt rim is optional. No syrup in this base recipe.

How to make it

  1. Chill a coupe or Margarita glass. If using salt, moisten half of the outer rim with lime and lightly dip it in fine salt, keeping salt out of the bowl.
  2. Juice the lime just before mixing. Measure all three ingredients into a shaker and fill with ice.
  3. Shake for about 10–15 seconds until thoroughly chilled, then strain into the prepared glass without ice. Fine-strain as well if you prefer fewer ice chips.

Flavour & texture

Lime brings freshness and acidity; orange liqueur adds peel aroma and sweetness. Blanco tequila may contribute cooked agave, grassy or peppery notes, depending on the bottle. The texture is lighter than a cream cocktail, with a citrus-led finish. Salt changes the balance but should not dominate it.

How to taste it

  1. Smell before sipping: can you distinguish lime and orange peel from the tequila? Hold the stem to keep the drink cool.
  2. Take the first small sip from the unsalted rim. Follow the opening acidity, the sweetness in the middle and the agave in the finish.
  3. Try the salted side next and compare. As the drink warms slightly, revisit the aroma and finish; distinguish pleasant citrus bite from an overly sharp balance.

A small adjustment

Limes and orange liqueurs vary. Make the base recipe first, then change only one variable next time: a little more lime for a sharper drink, or 5 ml of 1:1 sugar syrup if it is too tart. These are personal adjustments, not the IBA formula. Note the tequila, liqueur, juice quantity and shaking time so you can repeat a balance you like.
